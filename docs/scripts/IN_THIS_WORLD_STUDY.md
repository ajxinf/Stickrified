## 1. Hooks: the rule is the hook, said in one breath

- ref1 0.06 s: "In this town, everybody goes out partying **except me**." The world rule and the narrator's place
  in it (the outsider) in 9 words. The "except me" is the curiosity: why not you?
- ref2 0.10 s: "In this world, your biggest fear becomes a real monster that follows you everywhere, **and mine has
  been trying to take my little sister for six years**." Rule (generic "you"), then the personal stake, a person
  to lose and a number, in the same sentence.
- ref3 0.14 s: "In this world, everyone lives on a train that never stops." 11 words, one image. The next sentence
  ("See, a hundred years ago, the entire planet froze...") makes it a survival rule: "if the train ever stops, we all die."

All three open on the rule, not on a strange act; the rule itself is the strangest image. None needs a second idea
to understand the first. Ep 2 opened on an act ("You're punching the edge of the screen.") whose explanation took
three rules and ~25 s ("In this world" video, the red bar, the loop, the narrator, captions coming true); the user
called the result "all over the place".

## 2. Immersion: a person with a wound, not a premise

- **A name, an age, a lack**: "My name is Logan, and I've never seen the outside world." (32.7 s); "My name is
  Ethan. I'm thirteen, and my biggest fear is losing my little sister, Lily." (22.0 s). The lack is what the ending
  pays: Logan's mom touches a real flower; Ethan's fear turns out to protect Lily.
- **A memory that makes it personal**: "When I was seven, she disappeared at a shopping mall. We searched for four
  hours before finding her asleep inside a clothing store." (25.5-30.5 s). Concrete, ordinary, and planted for the
  final twist.
- **One emotionally loaded object**: the monster "carries an empty pink backpack. The exact same backpack Lily was
  wearing when she disappeared." (34.4-41.9 s). The beanie in ref1 ("Who's the kid in the beanie?"). The object turns
  a creature into a feeling.
- **The everyday people of the world, named**: "My best friend Kevin... My neighbor Linda... Even my grandma goes
  dancing on Tuesdays" (14.7-18.0 s); "My neighbor is terrified of heights, so a floating cliff follows him
  everywhere, and my dad is scared of losing money, so a giant wallet with teeth chases him around town" (11.0 s).
  The rule is shown working on ordinary people before the story starts.
- **Generic "you" only for the rule**: "If you're scared of spiders, a giant spider follows you to school." The refs
  use "you" to pull the viewer into the rule ("this would happen to YOU"), then "I" for the story.

## 3. Story progression

- **World tour by three escalating examples** right after the hook, with "You see," / "See,": pool parties, pizza
  parties, a birthday party for the mayor's goldfish; spider, floating cliff, wallet with teeth; the rich (pools,
  gardens), the workers, "And the poor? We live in the very last carriages." The third is the funniest or the
  personal one. Each takes 2-5 s.
- **The one constraint, stated plainly**: "But there's one rule: Your fear monster can never leave you." (17.8 s);
  "if the train ever stops, we all die" (12.6 s). The climax breaks or inverts exactly this.
- **Routine, then the break**: "Every morning, it stands outside our house. Every night, it watches Lily..." ->
  "Then last Friday, something happened." (51.9 s). "Every day, we get one tiny portion of food. But one morning,
  the guards tell my mom there's nothing left." (36-39 s). The routine makes the break felt.
- **Curiosity gaps right before reveals** (mini-hooks), each paid within ~2 s: "and what I see next makes me
  furious" (55 s), "and that's when I notice something strange" (72 s), "Then I noticed something." (127 s), "Then it
  did something I'd never seen before." (139 s), "The next night... something was wrong." (ref1 48 s).
- **Escalation by obstacles, in fragments**: "Then we reached the emergency exit. Locked. I pulled the handle.
  Nothing. Behind us, the monster was getting closer." (82.5-87 s). Sound words at the peak: "boom", "crack",
  "Boom, boom, boom. Every step shook the building."
- **The twist reframes the rule or the villain, with a planted detail**: the monster was protecting her, not taking
  her (it "reaches toward her whenever she goes somewhere alone" was planted at 46 s); the frozen world was a lie
  ("The government says" at 12.6 s planted the doubt); the party town turns into a grinding town because of the one
  kid who practised. Ref2 then stacks a second twist on a second plant (the mall disappearance -> "I wasn't the only
  one who came back").
- **No exposition dumps**: every fact is a picture (a wallet with teeth, a thousand carriages, fifty points). The
  backstory is two sentences.

## 4. Pacing and retention

- Short declaratives are the base (median 5-9 words), with longer sentences only to set up (the world tour) or to
  pile up a chase. Fragments ("Locked." "Nothing." "The monster.") only at peaks. Ep 2 was 53 % fragments: choppy all
  the way, so the peaks had nothing to stand out against.
- New information every 2-4 s, not new jokes: the refs carry 1-3 jokes in a whole video (the goldfish, the wallet
  with teeth), and the comedy comes from the world's logic. Our gag-every-5-8-s rule is about pictures; the picture
  can be a reveal, not a joke.
- Retention devices: the rule (why?), the stake (will she be OK?), the routine (when will it break?), the
  curiosity gaps (what did he see?), the planted detail (the backpack), the constraint (what if it stops?).

## 5. Style

- Plain, conversational American English; contractions; concrete nouns. Long words are concrete things
  ("basketball", "auditorium", "carriages", "restaurants"), not abstractions: 6.8-10 % of words have 8+ letters
  (our checker warned at 7 %; raised to 10 % for concrete nouns, abstract words still banned by the skill).
- Present tense in ref3 ("I sneak into a maintenance tunnel"), past tense in ref1 and ref2's story part; the
  narration never comments on itself, never explains why the rule exists beyond one sentence.
- Dialogue at emotional turns only, short, from people who matter: "Lily, run." "Ethan, I'm scared." "When did you
  get so good?" / "While you were out partying."
- A sound motif (ref1): "Hush, hush, hush" returns every chapter (2.3, 20.9, 24.4, 36.2 s) as the sound of the
  narrator practising alone; at 54.3 s "Then I heard it from every single window" turns it into the payoff.

## 6. Endings

- ref1: irony + callback to a throwaway: everyone now practises, so "I'm the only one who's free on a Friday night,
  so I threw a party. The only guest was the mayor's goldfish. He's actually a pretty good dancer." The goldfish was
  a joke in sentence 3.
- ref2: emotional reversal (the monster saved them, then hides behind him, terrified), then a cliffhanger built on
  the first memory ("I wasn't the only one who came back") and "To be continued" (it runs 167 s: a Part 1).
- ref3: the lack is filled, physically: "She kneels down and touches a real flower... I thought I'd never see the
  sun again. And for the first time in our lives, we're finally free." It answers "I've never seen the outside world".

## 7. Gap analysis: our "In this world" scripts

1. **Too many rules.** Ep 2 asked the viewer to learn three magic rules plus a jar mechanic in 25 s; every reference
   has one rule and at most one constraint. Lie-counter (one rule: your lie count floats over your head) is our best
   script for the same reason.
2. **A premise, not a person.** Ep 2's "you" wants out of a loop; nobody he loves is at stake and there is no wound.
   The references' engine is a relationship (a sister, a mom, the whole town vs the kid).
3. **No world tour.** We go from the rule straight into the plot; the references spend 10-15 s showing the rule in
   ordinary lives (three escalating examples) so the viewer lives in the world before the story starts.
4. **Jokes over story.** Our scripts put a gag on every line; the references let the world's logic be funny and keep
   the narration on what happens next.
5. **Choppy rhythm.** 53 % fragments in ep 2 vs 8-41 %: the punches stop landing because everything is a punch.
6. **Few explicit turns and curiosity gaps.** One "Then" in ep 2 vs 3-8; no "something happened" gaps.
7. **Meta and abstract worlds.** Captions, narrators and progress bars need explaining; the references' worlds are
   made of parties, fear, food and family, which need none.
8. **Person.** The references are first person with a named narrator; ours are second person by channel rule
   (CLAUDE.md). The intimacy can be kept in second person by giving "you" an age, a name for the people around
   "you", and a wound in one line ("You're thirteen, and you once lost your little sister at the mall for four
   hours."). The user decided (2026-11-16): keep "you".
